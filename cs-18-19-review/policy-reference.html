Public Policy Reference

Who the evidence represents

Hearing testimony describes the experiences of the people who attended. Broader outreach and representative evidence can reveal who was missed.

Comparing policy costs and benefits

A policy can improve one outcome while creating costs elsewhere. Compare alternatives and assess effects on each group, including public revenue, affordability, and access.

Input before a rule takes effect

When an agency requests public comments on a proposed rule, participants can submit evidence and recommend specific changes. The agency evaluates relevant evidence within its legal authority.

Outputs and outcomes

Outputs count activity, such as riders served, loans approved, or participants enrolled. Outcomes measure whether the underlying goal improved. Use a baseline and compare results for affected groups.

Intended and unintended effects

An intended effect is part of the stated policy goal. A change outside that goal is unintended. Monitor spillovers into nearby places and changes for different groups.

Authority and implementation

A proposal states an intended action. Implementation requires authority, funding, and the people or procedures that carry it out. A court ruling can change how an existing law is applied.

Problems that cross borders

Neighboring jurisdictions can share monitoring data and coordinate standards when actions in one area affect people elsewhere.

Taking evidence to the decision maker

Public testimony is most useful when it reaches the body responsible for the decision while changes are still possible. Documents and specific remedies help officials evaluate a request.

Choosing a safety investment

Compare crash history, existing conditions, expected injuries prevented, and costs. Consider impacts on users who depend on the affected roads, lanes, or sidewalks.
